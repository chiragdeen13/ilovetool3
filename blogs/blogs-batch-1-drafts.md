### Body

Most people in Pakistan open their electricity bill, look at the total, and pay it. The rest of the bill — the units, the slabs, the taxes and adjustments — goes unread. That is a pity, because the bill itself tells you exactly where your money went, and what you can change next month.

This guide explains each part of a typical bill in plain language, and shows how to estimate your next bill before it arrives.

### What "units" actually means

Your meter measures electricity in kilowatt-hours, usually written as kWh. On your bill these are called "units". One unit means you used 1,000 watts for one hour. A 100-watt bulb running for 10 hours uses exactly one unit. An air conditioner can use 15 to 25 units in a single day, which is why summer bills jump so sharply.

Your bill shows two meter readings: the previous reading and the current reading. The difference between them is the number of units you consumed this month. If that number looks wrong, check it against your own meter before anything else — a wrong reading is the most common billing complaint, and it is the easiest to prove.

### How slabs work, and why heavy use costs more per unit

Electricity in Pakistan is charged in slabs. The first block of units in a month is charged at a lower rate per unit, and each higher block is charged at a higher rate. So two households do not pay the same average rate: a home that uses 150 units pays a lower average per unit than a home that uses 600 units, because a bigger share of the second home's usage falls in the expensive slabs.

This is also why one extra appliance can feel so expensive. It is not just the cost of its own units — it can push some of your existing usage into a higher slab. Tariff rates change from time to time, so treat any rate you remember from last year as out of date, and use the calculator linked below for an estimate rather than an old rate card.

### The other lines on your bill

Beyond the energy charges, most bills carry a few more lines. A fixed or meter-related charge appears every month regardless of usage. Government taxes are applied on top of the energy charges. You may also see a fuel price adjustment, which reflects changes in the cost of generating electricity, and sometimes arrears or instalments from earlier bills. None of these are calculated from your units in the same way as the slab charges, which is why two bills with the same units can still differ slightly.

You do not need to wait for the bill to know roughly what is coming. Note your meter reading today, note it again a week later, and multiply the weekly units by four for a rough monthly figure. It is an estimate, not your distribution company's final bill, but it is close enough to warn you before a shock month.

### Worked example: reading a bill in one minute

Suppose your bill shows a previous reading of 12,400 and a current reading of 12,700. Subtract: 12,700 minus 12,400 equals 300 units for the month. Now look at how those 300 units are split across slabs, then check the fixed charges and taxes below them. If the units match your meter, the bill is almost certainly correct, and the only way to reduce next month's bill is to reduce units — which brings us to saving.

### Five ways to genuinely reduce your bill

First, the air conditioner: raising its temperature by even two degrees and using a fan with it can cut its units noticeably. Second, old fridges and water pumps are silent heavy users; a fridge with a damaged door seal runs far more than it should. Third, switch the lights you use most to LED bulbs, which use a fraction of the power of old bulbs. Fourth, iron clothes in one batch instead of a few pieces at a time. Fifth, unplug chargers and devices on standby — each one is small, but across a house they add up to real units every month.

### Common mistakes

The biggest mistake is comparing your total with a neighbour's total without comparing units — different usage, different slabs, different bill. Another is ignoring the meter reading and assuming the bill is always right; estimated readings do happen, and they are corrected only if you point them out. A third is buying a "bill saver" device that promises to cut your bill by magic. Your meter measures real energy used. No plug-in device changes that, and some of them are unsafe.

### Frequently asked questions

Q: Why is my summer bill double my winter bill? A: Cooling is the main reason. Air conditioners use many units per day, and higher usage also pushes you into more expensive slabs, so the bill rises faster than the units alone suggest.

Q: What should I check first if my bill looks too high? A: The meter readings. Compare the current reading on the bill with your actual meter. If they do not match, contact your distribution company with a photo of the meter.

Q: Can a calculator give me my exact bill? A: No. It gives an estimate based on units and the usual slab structure. Your final bill depends on your provider's current tariff, taxes and adjustments, which change over time.

Q: Does using less electricity always save money in the same proportion? A: Not exactly, because of slabs. Cutting usage that falls in a high slab saves more per unit than cutting usage in a low slab, which is another reason heavy users benefit most from saving.

### Body

Buying a used phone is a good way to save money, and also a good way to buy a problem. One free check takes less than a minute and rules out an entire class of problems: checking whether the phone's IMEI number is valid.

An IMEI is a 15-digit number that identifies a mobile phone the way a chassis number identifies a car. Every genuine phone has one, and no two phones should share one. Here is how to find it, check it, and understand what the result actually proves.

### How to find your IMEI

The fastest way is to open the phone app and dial *#06#. The IMEI appears on screen immediately, with no call made and no charge. You can also find it in the phone's Settings, under About Phone or a similar section, on the SIM tray on many models, and on the original box or purchase receipt. If you are buying a used phone, ask the seller to show you the IMEI on the phone itself using *#06# — not just on a box, because a box can belong to a different phone.

If the phone shows two IMEIs, that is normal for a dual-SIM phone. Check both, one at a time.

### What makes an IMEI valid

A valid IMEI has exactly 15 digits. The first 8 digits are called the TAC, which identifies the model and maker. The next 6 digits are the serial number for that model. The final digit is a check digit, calculated from the other 14 digits using the Luhn algorithm — the same kind of check used for many card numbers. If even one digit is mistyped, the check digit will not match, and the number fails validation.

That is exactly what the IMEI Validator on ilovetool3.com checks. Enter the 15 digits, and it tells you whether the number is structurally valid: https://ilovetool3.com/imei-validator/

### Worked example

Take a 15-digit IMEI and enter it into the validator. If the check digit matches the Luhn calculation, the tool reports the number as valid. Change a single middle digit and try again, and the same tool will report it as invalid — not because the phone changed, but because the number itself is no longer a possible IMEI. This is a useful habit before any purchase: type the number carefully, digit by digit, and re-check it if it fails once, because a typing error is the most common reason for a false failure.

### What a validity check proves — and what it does not

This is the part most people get wrong, so be clear about it. A validity check proves that the number is a structurally possible IMEI. It does not prove the phone is genuine, it does not prove it is not stolen, it does not prove it is under warranty, and it does not prove the phone's model from the number alone. For stolen or blocked status, you need your network operator or your country's device registry, and for warranty you need the manufacturer. A validity check is a first filter, not a full background check.

### Red flags when buying used

Walk away, or at least pause, if the seller will not show the IMEI on the phone itself, if the IMEI on the phone does not match the IMEI on the box, if the number fails validation after careful retyping, or if the phone's settings show a different IMEI from the *#06# screen. Any of these can have an innocent explanation, but together with a rushed sale they are classic signs of a tampered or misrepresented phone.

### Common mistakes

The most common mistake is checking the IMEI from a photo of the box instead of the phone. Another is typing the number from memory instead of copying it digit by digit. A third is treating "valid" as "safe to buy" — validity is necessary, but it is not sufficient on its own. Finally, never share your own phone's full IMEI publicly, for example in a sale listing photo. It is an identifier for your device, and there is no reason to publish it.

### Frequently asked questions

Q: Is a 15-digit number starting with any digits automatically valid? A: No. Length alone is not enough. The final check digit must match the Luhn calculation for the first 14 digits, which is what the validator tests.

Q: My phone has two IMEIs. Is that a problem? A: No. Dual-SIM phones normally have one IMEI per SIM slot. Validate each one separately.

Q: Can a valid IMEI still belong to a stolen phone? A: Yes. Validity only checks the number's structure. Stolen or blocked status is a separate check with your operator or device registry.

Q: What is the TAC? A: The first 8 digits of the IMEI, called the Type Allocation Code. It identifies the model and manufacturer, which is why two phones of the same model share the same opening digits but differ later.

### Body

Zakat is one of the most asked-about calculations every year, and also one of the easiest to get wrong — usually because people are unsure what counts, or they guess the amount instead of working it out. This guide walks through the standard method step by step, with a worked example you can follow with your own figures.

A note before we start: this is a general educational guide to the calculation, not a personal religious ruling. If your situation is unusual — business stock, debts, agricultural produce, or mixed assets — a local scholar can advise on your specific case.

### The basic idea in one paragraph

Zakat is normally 2.5 percent of a Muslim's zakatable wealth that has been held for one lunar year, provided that wealth is above a minimum threshold called the nisab. The nisab is tied to the value of gold or silver, so its amount in rupees changes as metal prices change. That is why a fixed rupee figure from a few years ago should not be used — check the current nisab, or use a calculator that lets you work from your own figures.

### Step 1: List what counts

Start by listing the wealth you have held for a full lunar year. For most people this means: cash in hand, money in bank accounts, gold and silver (including jewellery held as wealth), and goods held for trade if you run a business. Your family home, the car you drive, your clothes, and your furniture do not count, because they are for personal use, not wealth held as an asset.

### Step 2: Subtract what you owe

Debts that are due now, and immediate living expenses, are subtracted before you calculate. The point of this step is simple: Zakat is due on net wealth, not on money that is already owed to someone else. Be honest here, but do not subtract long-term debts in full if only the current instalment is due — this is one of the points where personal advice helps.

### Step 3: Compare with the nisab

Compare your net figure with the nisab. If it is below the nisab, no Zakat is due on that wealth this year. If it is above, Zakat is due on the whole zakatable amount at 2.5 percent. Because the nisab moves with gold and silver prices, check a current value rather than relying on memory.

### Step 4: Calculate 2.5 percent

Two and a half percent is the same as dividing by 40. So a net zakatable amount of Rs 1,000,000 gives Zakat of Rs 1,000,000 divided by 40, which is Rs 25,000. You can check this on the Percentage Calculator, or use the dedicated Zakat Calculator on ilovetool3.com, which walks through the same steps: https://ilovetool3.com/zakat-calculator/

### Worked example

Suppose Sara has Rs 800,000 in savings, gold worth Rs 300,000, and an immediate debt of Rs 100,000. Her zakatable wealth is Rs 800,000 plus Rs 300,000 minus Rs 100,000, which equals Rs 1,000,000. Assuming this is above the nisab and has been held for a lunar year, her Zakat is 2.5 percent of Rs 1,000,000, which is Rs 25,000. The arithmetic is the easy part — the care belongs in steps 1 and 2, listing honestly and subtracting only what is genuinely owed.

### Common mistakes

The most common mistake is paying Zakat on total income instead of on wealth held for a year — salary is not itself zakatable until it is saved and held. Another is forgetting gold jewellery that is held as wealth. A third is using an old nisab figure, which can wrongly put someone above or below the threshold. And a fourth is guessing: a ten-minute calculation beats a guessed amount every time, for the giver's peace of mind as much as for accuracy.

### Frequently asked questions

Q: Is Zakat 2.5 percent of my salary? A: No. It is 2.5 percent of zakatable wealth held for a lunar year above the nisab, not a percentage of income. Salary that is spent does not accumulate Zakat; salary that is saved and held does, as part of your wealth.

Q: Do I pay Zakat on my house or car? A: Not on the home you live in or the car you use. Personal-use items are not zakatable. Property held for sale or rent as an investment is treated differently, and that is worth asking a scholar about.

Q: What if my wealth crosses the nisab during the year? A: The standard rule counts wealth held for a full lunar year. If your situation changed mid-year, note the date carefully and ask for guidance rather than guessing.

Q: Can I just use a calculator and be done? A: A calculator does the arithmetic reliably, which is exactly what it is for. It cannot decide for you what counts in your personal situation, so treat its result as the calculation step, not the whole answer.

### Body

Most password advice fails for the same reason: it tells you to create something impossible to remember, so you write it on a sticky note or reuse it everywhere. A strong password system has to survive real life. Here is one that does.

### Why length beats cleverness

Password strength is mostly about length. A short password, however clever, can be guessed quickly by a computer trying millions of combinations. Each extra character multiplies the guesses needed. That is why a 16-character password is dramatically stronger than an 8-character one, even if the 8-character one looks complicated. When you set a password, length is the single most useful choice you make.

### The passphrase method

If you must remember a password yourself, use a passphrase: four or five random words strung together, for example "correct horse battery staple" style combinations of words you would not normally put in one sentence. A passphrase is long, which makes it strong, and it is made of words, which makes it memorable. Do not use a famous quote, a song lyric, or words about yourself — birthdays, pet names, and team names are the first things an attacker guesses.

### One password per important site

Reusing one password everywhere is the most dangerous habit of all. When any single site leaks its passwords — and large leaks happen regularly — every other account using the same password is open too. Your email password matters most of all, because email is how most other accounts are reset. Give your email, your bank, and your main social accounts each a password used nowhere else.

### Use a password manager, and let a generator do the random part

Humans are bad at randomness, which is why generated passwords are better than invented ones. ### Turn on two-factor authentication

A password, however strong, can still be stolen. Two-factor authentication means a stolen password alone is not enough — an attacker also needs a code from your phone or an authenticator app. Turn it on for email, banking, and social accounts first. It is the single highest-value security step after using unique passwords, and it takes minutes.

### Common mistakes

Using the same password everywhere is the biggest one. Next is using personal information — names, dates, and places connected to you. Another is changing passwords on a schedule to slightly different versions, like adding a "1" then a "2", which is easy to guess once one version leaks. And never share a password in a chat message or email "just this once" — that copy lives forever in someone's inbox.

### Frequently asked questions

Q: Is a long simple password better than a short complex one? A: Usually yes. Length increases the guessing work far more than swapping a letter for a symbol does. A long passphrase beats a short jumble in most cases.

Q: Should I change my passwords regularly? A: Not on a fixed schedule. Change a password when there is a reason: a site reports a breach, you reused it somewhere, or you shared it. Changing to a weaker "next version" on a schedule helps no one.

Q: Are password generators safe? A: A generator that runs in your browser and does not send or store your password is safe to use for creating one. The risk is not the generation, it is where you store the result afterwards — use a password manager, not a note on your desk.

Q: What if I forget a generated password? A: That is what the account's reset process is for, and it is exactly why your email account needs its own strong password and two-factor authentication. A forgotten password is an inconvenience; a reused password is a breach.
