### Body

Almost every assignment, article, and application comes with a word limit, and almost every writer has the same two problems: not knowing the count, and not knowing what counts. This guide explains how word counting actually works, so you can hit a limit honestly instead of guessing.

### What counts as a word

The usual rule is simple: a word is a group of characters separated by spaces. By that rule, the sentence "The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog." has 9 words. Most counters follow this rule, which is why pasting the same text into two different counters usually gives the same word count.

The small differences come from edge cases. Hyphenated words may count as one word or two depending on the counter. Numbers usually count as words. A standalone symbol, like a lone dash, may or may not count. None of this matters much for a long essay, but it explains why a short text can differ by one or two words between tools. If a limit is strict, check it with the same counter your teacher or editor will use, and keep a small margin.

### Words are not the only number that matters

A good counter also shows characters, with and without spaces, sentences, and paragraphs. Characters matter when a form has a character limit instead of a word limit — social media bios and application boxes often do. Sentences matter because very long sentences are hard to read. And reading time, usually estimated at around 200 words per minute for an average adult reader, tells you how long your piece takes to read: a 600-word article takes roughly 3 minutes, a 1,500-word essay roughly 7 to 8 minutes. These are estimates, not exact measures, but they are useful when you are writing for readers with limited time.

There is no sign-up and nothing is saved — the counting runs in your browser. That makes it handy for drafts you do not want to upload anywhere, like an unfinished assignment or a job application.

### Worked example: checking a short paragraph

Take this sentence: "The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog." Counted the usual way, by splitting on spaces, that is 9 words. Now imagine a 300-word essay draft that must be under 250 words. You need to cut about 50 words, which is roughly one sixth of the draft. The honest way to do that is to cut whole sentences that repeat a point, not to delete every second adjective. Padding works the other way too: if you are 50 words short, add an example or a supporting point, not filler phrases. Teachers and editors can both spot padding, and it weakens the piece more than a slightly short count does.

### Common mistakes

The most common mistake is counting by pages instead of words. Pages vary with font size, margins, and spacing, so "two pages" can mean anything from 400 to 1,000 words. Another mistake is trusting a word processor's count without checking whether it includes footnotes, text boxes, or headers — some do, some do not, and assignment rules differ on this. A third mistake is writing to the limit instead of to the point: a clear 480-word essay beats a padded 500-word essay every time.

### Frequently asked questions

Q: Do hyphenated words count as one word or two? A: It depends on the counter. Most space-based counters count a hyphenated word as one word. If the limit is strict, check the rule your teacher or editor uses.

Q: Do numbers count as words? A: In most counters, yes. A year like 2026 or a figure like 1,000 normally counts as one word each.

Q: How long does it take to read 1,000 words? A: Roughly 5 minutes at an average reading speed of about 200 words per minute. Slow, careful reading takes longer.

Q: Should I include the title and references in my count? A: Only if the assignment says so. When in doubt, ask — this is the single most common source of word-count disputes.

### Body

A loan advertisement always shows the monthly instalment in big print and the total cost in small print. The instalment is called EMI — Equated Monthly Instalment — and understanding how it is calculated is the difference between borrowing with open eyes and discovering the cost years later.

### What EMI actually is

EMI is a fixed monthly payment that repays both the principal (the amount you borrowed) and the interest over the loan's tenure. The payment stays the same every month, but what is inside it changes: early payments are mostly interest, and later payments are mostly principal. This happens because interest each month is charged on the remaining balance, which is largest at the start and shrinks as you repay.

That is why the first year of a long loan can feel like nothing is being repaid. It is being repaid — just slowly, because most of each early payment is interest on a large balance.

### The three inputs that decide your EMI

Only three things decide an EMI: how much you borrow, the annual interest rate, and the tenure. Borrow more, and the EMI rises. Pay a higher rate, and the EMI rises. Stretch the tenure, and the monthly EMI falls — but the total interest you pay rises, because you are in debt for longer. A longer tenure is not cheaper borrowing; it is smaller monthly payments at a larger total cost. Any honest comparison of two loans must look at total interest, not just the monthly figure.

One practical note about the calculator on this site: its tenure field is in months, not years. A 5-year loan is entered as 60 months, and a 20-year loan as 240 months. Entering years by mistake is the easiest way to get a wildly wrong answer.

### Worked example: the standard EMI formula

The standard EMI formula is: EMI = P × r × (1 + r)^n ÷ ((1 + r)^n − 1), where P is the principal, r is the monthly interest rate, and n is the number of monthly payments.

Take a loan of Rs 1,000,000 at 12 percent annual interest for 60 months. The monthly rate r is 12 percent divided by 12, which is 0.01. Working through the formula gives a monthly EMI of about Rs 22,244. Over 60 months you pay about Rs 1,334,640 in total, which means about Rs 334,640 of it is interest — roughly a third on top of what you borrowed. This is a formula calculation shown for teaching; try the same figures in the Loan Calculator to compare, and check your own loan's actual rate and charges, because processing fees and taxes are not part of this basic formula: https://ilovetool3.com/loan-calculator/

### How to check a loan offer in five minutes

First, enter the exact principal, rate, and tenure in months into the calculator and note the EMI and total interest. Second, ask the lender for the total cost including processing fees — a slightly lower rate with a large fee can be worse than a slightly higher rate with no fee. Third, check whether the rate is fixed or floating: a floating rate can rise, and your EMI or tenure will rise with it. Fourth, ask about prepayment: some loans charge for paying early, which matters if you plan to clear the loan ahead of time. Fifth, never borrow based on the EMI alone — check that the EMI fits comfortably in your monthly budget after essentials, not just on paper.

### Common mistakes

The biggest mistake is choosing the longest tenure just because the EMI looks small, without noticing the total interest. Another is entering tenure in years into a months field, or the reverse. A third is ignoring fees and insurance bundled into the loan, which raise the real cost above the advertised rate. And a fourth is borrowing the maximum offered rather than the amount actually needed — the bank's maximum is not a recommendation.

### Frequently asked questions

Q: Why does my balance fall so slowly in the first year? A: Because early EMIs are mostly interest on the full balance. As the balance falls, more of each EMI goes to principal, so repayment speeds up later.

Q: Does a longer tenure save money? A: No. It lowers the monthly EMI but increases total interest, because interest is charged for more months.

Q: Is the EMI from a calculator exact? A: It is exact for the basic formula with the figures you enter. Your lender's final figure can differ if fees, taxes, or a different rate calculation apply, so treat it as a strong estimate and confirm with the lender.

Q: What happens if I pay extra one month? A: Extra payments usually reduce the principal, which reduces future interest. Check whether your lender charges a prepayment fee first, and confirm the extra payment is applied to principal.

### Body

BMI is probably the most quoted health number in the world, and also one of the most misunderstood. It is a single number from your height and weight, and people treat it as a verdict on their health. It is not. It is a screening tool — useful for populations and as a starting point for individuals, but never the whole story.

This is general health information, not medical advice. For personal health decisions, a doctor who knows your history is the right person to ask.

### How BMI is calculated

BMI stands for Body Mass Index. The formula is simple: BMI = weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. So a person who weighs 80 kg and is 1.75 metres tall has a BMI of 80 ÷ (1.75 × 1.75), which is 80 ÷ 3.0625, which equals about 26.1.

Try your own figures on the BMI Calculator on ilovetool3.com: https://ilovetool3.com/bmi-calculator/

### What the categories mean

The widely used adult categories are: below 18.5 is underweight, 18.5 to 24.9 is the healthy range, 25 to 29.9 is overweight, and 30 and above is obese. These bands come from population studies linking BMI ranges to health risks on average. They describe risk across large groups, not a diagnosis for any one person.

Our worked example above, at 26.1, falls in the overweight band. That does not by itself mean the person is unhealthy — it means their weight-for-height is above the range associated with lowest average risk, and it is worth looking at the fuller picture below.

### What BMI does not tell you

BMI cannot tell muscle from fat. A heavily muscled athlete can have a high BMI while carrying little body fat, and an older person can have a "healthy" BMI while carrying a high proportion of fat and little muscle. BMI also says nothing about where fat is carried — fat around the waist carries more risk than fat elsewhere — and nothing about fitness, diet, blood pressure, cholesterol, or blood sugar. Two people with the same BMI can have very different health profiles.

BMI is also less reliable for children and teenagers, who need age-based charts, and during pregnancy, where weight changes have their own guidance. For these groups, do not use the adult bands at all.

### How to use your BMI sensibly

Use it as a first check, then add context. If your BMI is outside the healthy range, look at your waist measurement, your activity level, your diet, and your family history, and consider a check-up rather than a crash diet. If your BMI is inside the healthy range but you are inactive with a poor diet, the number alone is not a clean bill of health either. Small, steady changes — regular walking, less sugary drinks, more vegetables, better sleep — matter more than chasing a single number quickly.

### Common mistakes

The most common mistake is treating BMI as a diagnosis or a grade. Another is comparing your BMI with someone of a different build, age, or sex as if the same number meant the same thing. A third is crash dieting to reach a band boundary by a deadline, which usually rebounds. And a fourth is ignoring the number entirely because it is imperfect — imperfect does not mean useless, it means it needs context.

### Frequently asked questions

Q: Is BMI accurate? A: It is accurate arithmetic from height and weight, but it is only a rough indicator of body fat and health risk. Accuracy of the number is not the same as completeness of the picture.

Q: Can a muscular person have an overweight BMI and be healthy? A: Yes. Muscle weighs more than fat for the same volume, so BMI overestimates fatness in very muscular people. That is a known limitation, not a fault in the measurement.

Q: Does BMI work for children? A: No, not with adult bands. Children and teenagers are assessed with age- and sex-based percentile charts instead.

Q: What should I do if my BMI is high? A: Do not panic and do not crash diet. Look at diet, activity, and sleep, consider a routine check-up, and get personal advice from a doctor, especially if you have other risk factors.

### Body

Every giveaway, classroom draw, and team raffle ends with the same awkward moment: someone has to pick a winner, and everyone else has to believe it was fair. The solution is not a better hat. It is a written method, decided before the draw, that anyone can check afterwards.

### Step 1: Number every entry before you draw

Write the entries in a list and number them from 1 upwards, in the order they arrived or in alphabetical order — either is fine, as long as the order is fixed before the draw and visible to everyone. If you have 50 entries, they are numbered 1 to 50. This step matters more than the draw itself, because a draw from an unclear list is unfair no matter how random the number is.

### Step 2: Set the generator to match your list

Set the Minimum to 1 and the Maximum to your number of entries — 50 for our example. If you are picking more than one winner, set "How Many Numbers?" to the number of winners, and keep the "No duplicates" option checked, so the same entry cannot win twice. Leave sorting off if you want the winners in draw order. Then generate. The numbers you get are your winners, matched against your numbered list.

### Step 3: Record the result immediately

Screenshot or write down the generated numbers, the date, and the settings you used, and share them with the group. A draw that is recorded is a draw that can be believed. If anyone questions it, you can show the list, the settings, and the result together. Decide the re-draw rule in advance too: if a winner is ineligible or does not respond within a stated time, do you draw the next number from the same generation, or generate again? Either rule is fair if it is announced before the draw, and neither is fair if it is invented after an unpopular result.

### Why a generator beats picking by hand

Picking names from a hat feels fair, but hands are not random: entries at the top get picked more, folded slips differ in size, and the person drawing may unconsciously favour a familiar name. A generator gives every number in the range an equal chance, and it does so repeatably in front of witnesses. It also scales — a hat works for 20 names, but not for 2,000.

One honest limitation: a browser generator like this one produces pseudo-random numbers, which are more than fair enough for giveaways, classrooms, and team draws. They are not suitable for cryptography, gambling systems, or anything where money or security depends on unpredictability. For a prize draw, that distinction does not matter; for a lottery or a security key, use a properly designed system instead.

### Worked example: a 50-entry giveaway, method only

Suppose a small shop runs a giveaway with 50 entries, numbered 1 to 50 in arrival order. The draw settings are: Minimum 1, Maximum 50, How Many Numbers 1, No duplicates checked. Generate once, and the number shown is the winner — match it to the list and announce both the number and the name. Because the result is random, this guide deliberately does not quote a fixed winning number: every generation gives a different result, and quoting one would be misleading. If a second prize exists, generate again with the winner removed, or set How Many Numbers to 2 before the first generation, and record which method you chose.

### Common mistakes

The biggest mistake is drawing before the entry list is final — late entries then have no fair chance. Another is re-generating until a preferred name appears, which is not a draw at all. A third is allowing duplicates when picking multiple winners, so one person can sweep the prizes. And a fourth is keeping the method secret: a fair draw done in private still looks unfair, so do it where entrants can see it or publish the record immediately.

### Frequently asked questions

Q: Is a random number generator really random? A: For giveaways, yes, effectively. Browser generators use pseudo-random algorithms that give every number an equal chance for practical purposes. They are not designed for cryptography or gambling, where stronger randomness is required.

Q: Should I sort the results? A: Only if you want winners listed in numerical order. For a draw, leave sorting off so winners stay in the order they were drawn.

Q: What if the same person wins twice? A: That should not happen if "No duplicates" is checked. If it is not checked, generate again with it checked, and announce the rule before you start.

Q: Can I use this for a money lottery? A: No. Money lotteries need regulated, audited systems and legal compliance. Use a generator only for informal giveaways, classrooms, and team draws.
